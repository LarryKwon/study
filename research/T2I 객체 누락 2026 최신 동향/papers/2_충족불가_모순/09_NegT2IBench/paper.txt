                                                                                                        Abstract
                                                    Text-to-image (T2I) models are judged by benchmarks that measure whether requested content appears, but these
                                                benchmarks largely overlook the complementary ability to satisfy negated constraints, for example, generating “a
                                                non-red cup.” Measuring negation raises challenges not faced by affirmation-based benchmarks and requires careful
                                                prompt and evaluation design. We introduce N EG T2IB ENCH, a benchmark of 4,800 prompts covering two attribute
                                                types and four relation categories. Prompts are organized by polarity: the number of positive statements that must hold
                                                and negated statements that must not, each ranging from 0 to 2. Varying the two independently separates the effect of
                                                negation from the effect of prompt complexity. Our detector-based scoring is reproducible, auditable, and pinpoints
                                                which requirement failed. On 600 images with three-annotator labels, it agrees with humans as closely as vision-
                                                language judges up to 30× larger, while using only a fraction of their GPU memory. Across eleven T2I models and
                                                211,200 images, nine score lower on a single negated statement than on a single positive one. Per-statement scoring
                                                reveals that the loss is largest for color and near zero for proximity, and that 41.5% of failed statements render exactly
                                                what the prompt forbids. Rendering what a prompt asks for and withholding what it forbids are distinct capabilities
                                                that an aggregate compositional score cannot distinguish. N EG T2IB ENCH measures the latter directly, providing a
                                                controlled testbed for diagnosing negation failures and developing methods to overcome them.



                                        1      Introduction
                                        While text-to-image (T2I) models now render increasingly complex prompts accurately enough for professional [1]
                                        and creative [2] applications, evaluating whether they respect what a prompt forbids is surprisingly difficult and under-
                                        explored. The frontier is judged by benchmarks like GenEval [3], T2I-CompBench [4], and DPG-Bench [5], which
                                        focus on challenging, dense prompts with multiple components that must each be correct. Though these benchmarks
                                        cover many important prompt-following aspects, they largely overlook an important capability: negation.
                                            Measuring negation raises challenges not faced by affirmation-based benchmarks, making it more nuanced than
                                        simply adding negated prompts to existing benchmarks. First, success can be accidental: cups rarely fly, so “a cup
                                        that is not flying” can produce a valid image even if not was ignored. Second, if object presence is not verified, a
                                        negated attribute can be satisfied by omitting the object rather than the attribute: “a non-wooden bench” may simply
                                        omit the bench. Third, negation inherits the difficulty of dense prompts; adding objects, like “a red cup” vs. “a red cup
                                        with no spoon” introduces multiple potential failures, so failures from negation must be disentangled from those from
                                        prompt complexity. Fourth, the evaluator can also introduce errors: when checking for forbidden elements, vision-
                                        language models may report absent content [6] or hallucinate [7], making evaluator errors difficult to distinguish from
                                        image-generation errors.
                                            We introduce N EG T2IB ENCH, a benchmark for measuring negation in text-to-image generation, designed around
                                        these four challenges. To reduce accidental success, an LLM [8] selects plausible attributes and relations, and fixed
                                        templates keep wording consistent across conditions. To prevent success by omission, evaluation requires every named

2    Related Work
Text-to-image models and compositional evaluation. Diffusion, flow-based, and autoregressive T2I models [10–
19] and closed systems [20–22] follow increasingly complex prompts. However, advances mostly show results for
whether requested content appears, not whether a specified attribute or relation is withheld. Distribution metrics such
as FID [23] and alignment scores such as CLIPScore [24] give coarse quality and prompt following scores. Like our
approach, GenEval [3] uses detectors to check objects, counts, colors, and positions, while PosEval [25] extends its
position task; N EG T2IB ENCH builds on GenEval’s prompt schema and PosEval’s spatial encoding. Other benchmarks
cover more attributes, relations, and longer prompts [4, 5, 26–29]. GenAI-Bench [30] and UniGenBench++ [31] in-
clude negation as a compositional skill or a grammar category, but neither separates attribute from relation negation or
pairs negated prompts with positive ones. Detector-based benchmarks also face template contamination [32], weak-
ening detector-human agreement as models improve [29], and sensitivity to wording [33]; we therefore report human
agreement by category and keep wording fixed across polarity conditions.
    Negation in text-to-image generation. T2I models still render what a prompt forbids, across several languages [34,
35]. NegGenBench [36] contains 200 prompt pairs and evaluates negative-prompt guidance: each prompt is paired
with a separate negative prompt naming the content to exclude, instead of expressing the negation in the prompt itself.
LMD [37] tests 100 scene-level prompts without a named object, scoring with an open-vocabulary detector and sup-
plying the denied object via a negative-prompt input. NegationCLIP [9] evaluates 107 negated prompts with an MLLM
judge, while NEG-TTOI [38] pairs a wanted object with an unwanted one in 2,000 prompts scored by GPT-4o. These
studies establish that exclusion is hard, but none compares positive and negated versions of attributes and relations
while requiring every named object to remain. A separate line of work steers generation away from unwanted content
with negative prompts or guidance [39–43]. While steering generation with negative prompts can exclude unwanted
content, it does not test whether the model follows a negation written in the prompt, which is what N EG T2IB ENCH
measures.
    Judging negation. Image-text and preference scores such as CLIPScore [24], BLIPScore [44], ImageReward [45],
PickScore [46], and HPSv2 [47] measure overall alignment, but performance on ordinary prompts does not indicate
negation reliability: CLIP-family models perform near chance on negated captions [48], VQA models perform much
worse on negated questions [49], and CC-Neg [50] provides negated captions for training. VQA-based methods
such as TIFA [51], which uses mPLUG [52], and VQAScore [53] verify prompt requirements directly, but VQA
judges, including Qwen3-VL [54] and InternVL3.5 [55], can hallucinate absent details [7, 56], which matters when
the evaluator must confirm that an attribute or relation is absent while the objects are present. Sammani et al. [57]
propose a decomposed MLLM judge for negated image retrieval, but in our validation such judges lose up to 18.8 κ
points when negations are phrased as negations (Section 4). We therefore score each element with dedicated perception
components and report human agreement by category and polarity.


3    The N EG T2IB ENCH Benchmark
Task formulation. N EG T2IB ENCH measures how models respond when attributes and relations are negated. It tests
both the effect of negation at a fixed number of statements and the effect of combining multiple statements in one
prompt. Each prompt comes with metadata listing required objects and statements. A statement’s polarity specifies
whether an attribute or relation must hold (positive) or must not hold (negated); all named objects remain required in
either case. The eight settings vary the number of positive and negated statements; we write a setting as P:N, so 1:1
contains one of each, and call P+N its load. To capture diverse forms of negation in prompts, we cover two attribute
and four relation categories (Table 1), illustrated by the examples in Figure 1. Relation prompts link one shared subject
to a different target for each statement, so M statements name M + 1 objects and changing polarity leaves both counts

     Benchmark construction. We use GenEval’s color vocabulary and adapt the position rules [3], select material
values from OVAD [59], and follow PosEval’s relation encoding [25]. An LLM [8] selects combinations of objects,
attributes, and relations from fixed lists. It keeps combinations that are plausible or unusual but possible to depict, so
each negated statement denies something that could reasonably appear. Rule-based templates turn these selections into
prompts with consistent wording. We balance different types of objects, attributes, and relations by favoring the ones
that are used least often. The benchmark contains 4,800 prompts, with 100 for each combination of six categories and
eight settings. Each category includes all 79 eligible object classes. Appendix A.1 gives the settings, selection rules,
and templates; Appendices C.1 and A.2 give the full vocabularies and distributions.

3.1    Detector-based evaluation
We check each prompt requirement separately to identify why an image fails. This could be a missing object, an
incorrect count, or a violated attribute or relation. Each category uses the same verification rule (Appendix Table 14),
making decisions reproducible and comparable across generators. Checking that every named object is present first
prevents a model from satisfying a negated statement by omitting the object it refers to. Figure 2 illustrates this
evaluation pipeline: both objects are present, but the horse is behind the computer mouse, violating the negated
relation. We use RF-DETR-XL [60] to detect objects, SAM 2 [61] to refine their masks, and Depth-Anything V2 [62]
to estimate depth for front-behind relations.
     Statement decisions. For color and material, SigLIP 2 [63] scores the masked object crop against the category’s
values. For color, a positive attribute passes if its score is within 0.5 of the highest score; a negated attribute passes
if its score is more than 0.5 below it. This prevents a near-tied negated value from being treated as absent. For
material, the top-scoring value decides both polarities. For relations, positive statements pass when their rule holds,
and negated statements pass when it does not. Directional, comparative, and depth rules can return no relation when
measurements are ambiguous (a dead zone). Positive statements then fail, while the negated one passes because the
denied relation was not found. Table 14 defines the rules and final thresholds. Appendix C.1 gives the full equations
and implementation details, and Appendix E.2 reports the frequency of ambiguous passes. Appendix D compares
detectors, mask refinement, and relation thresholds on a small subset of 140 images with a clear human-majority
verdict, from 600 annotated images.
     Metrics. Image accuracy is the fraction of generated images that satisfy the full prompt: all required objects
appear with valid counts, and every positive and negated statement passes. Prompt accuracy is the fraction of prompts
for which at least one of four generated images is correct. The first measures success per image; the second measures

4    Validation of N EG T2IB ENCH’s Evaluation
Since N EG T2IB ENCH scores images automatically, its verdicts matter only if they match human judgment. We test
this with a human study and compare N EG T2IB ENCH with other evaluators.
    Human reference. A pool of 36 annotators labeled 600 generated images, with three labels per image, answering
one yes/no question per object and per statement. We broke down each prompt into its objects and statements and
phrased each statement as a question about whether it holds: for “a non-wooden bench”, annotators say whether the
bench is wooden, and the correct answer is “no”. The majority vote on each element gives two references: a binary
image label, true only if every element is correct, and a continuous score, the fraction of correct elements. Some
images are ambiguous, so we also report an inter-annotator score (H. Inter-annotator), computed by scoring each
annotator against the majority of the other two (Appendix B.1). Evaluators are scored against the majority of all three
annotators, a less noisy reference, so they can exceed this value.
    Evaluators. We compare N EG T2IB ENCH (Section 3.1) with eight VQA judges: mPLUG [52], VQAScore [53],
Qwen2.5-VL-7B [64], InternVL3-8B [65], Qwen3-VL-8B and 32B [54], and InternVL3.5-8B and 38B [55]. Each
judge answers yes/no questions in three modes: one question for the whole prompt (whole), the annotators’ per-
element questions (pos.), and the same questions with negated statements asked in negated form (neg.). The probability
of the correct answer produces a continuous score, while an answer with a probability greater than 0.5 yields a binary
verdict. In per-element mode, an image passes only if every element receives the correct answer, similar to human
labeling. N EG T2IB ENCH runs one detector pass per image, so it has a single mode. We also test six image-text
similarity and reward scores, which have no binary form (see Appendix B.3).
    Agreement with humans. Table 2 reports Cohen’s κ [66], precision and recall against the binary label (per-
element mode for judges), and Kendall τ [67] against the continuous score. N EG T2IB ENCH reaches κ = 72.0,
above the human inter-annotator score (65.6), and τ = 65.1, above the best judge, Qwen3-VL-32B (63.3; bootstrap
intervals overlap, Table 7). On κ, only Qwen3-VL-32B scores higher (74.3), while the other seven judges score below
N EG T2IB ENCH on both measures. Size adds little: scaling Qwen3-VL from 8B to 32B gains 4.6 points of κ, and
InternVL3.5 from 8B to 38B gains 0.5. Similarity and reward scores reach at most τ = 27.7 and cannot tell whether
a negated requirement holds (Table 7). In Table 2, N EG T2IB ENCH has the highest recall of any evaluator (85.0 vs.
82.0 for the best judge) and trails it on precision (79.2 vs. 84.1), so it rejects fewer human-accepted images than any
judge: the failures we report are not an artifact of a strict evaluator (Appendix B.5).
    Negative phrasing effect. When we ask negated statements in negated form (“is the bench non-wooden?”) and
keep all other questions the same (∆ in Table 2), all judges lose 1.3 to 18.8 points of κ, and five of them lose 4.5 to
6.9 points of τ , including the largest models. Judge verdicts thus depend on wording, the very variable a negation

5    Benchmarking text-to-image models
We evaluate eleven T2I models on the full N EG T2IB ENCH corpus: BLIP3-o NEXT SFT 3B [19], FLUX.2-klein-9B
and FLUX.2-dev (4-bit) [13], HiDream-I1 (4-bit) [15], HunyuanImage-2.1 (distilled) [17], Janus-Pro-7B [18], Qwen-
Image [14], SD 3.5 Large [12], Z-Image-Turbo [16], and SD 1.5 [10] with and without negation-aware text encoder
NegationCLIP [9]. Each configuration produces four samples for 4,800 prompts: 19,200 images per configuration,
totaling 211,200 (Appendix E.1).
    Scoring uses Section 3.1’s evaluator: RF-DETR-XL [60] at presence/counting thresholds 0.03/0.5, SAM 2 masks [61]
for relations, SigLIP 2 [63] for color and material, and Depth-Anything V2 [62] for depth. Geometric cut points and at-
tribute confusion buffer are fitted on 140 human-annotated images, then fixed for the whole benchmark (Appendix D).
Prompts are sampled four times. Each model uses its native configuration: 4 to 50 steps, and default resolution from
3842 to 10242 . Appendix E.1 lists exact configurations.
    Table 3 reports accuracy by polarity, statement load, and semantic category. The strongest model, BLIP3-o NEXT,
reaches 31.4%. Scores vary by about ±0.7 points overall and ±2 points per subtask by chance alone (95% interval).
The top three models lie within 1.1 points, so overall score cannot separate them, but subtasks can. Janus-Pro-7B ties
FLUX.2-klein-9B fourth at 26.6% overall, yet first on negated single statement at 54.8%, ahead of BLIP3-o NEXT at
48.7%, and first on color at 39.8%. FLUX.2-dev (4-bit) is the opposite: first on the asserted single statement at 76.3%,
and 42.6% once negated. Accuracy on one negated statement spans 29.0 to 54.8%, a wider range than overall score.
Four models lead at least one subtask, and none leads more than three of eight polarity subtasks or two of six category
subtasks.

76.3% at Load 1 to 17.9% at Load 3 and 8.4% at Load 4. N EG T2IB ENCH exposes two separate difficulties: negation
is harder than assertion at equal load, and satisfying several constraints at once is hard whatever their polarity.
     Penalty depends on category. At Load 1, the drop is largest for the attributes: 53.7 points for color, from 85.0%
asserted to 31.3% denied, and 17.0 points for material. Relations lose less, 16.6 points for comparative, 14.6 for
directional. Proximity barely moves, 45.8% vs. 43.2%. Depth is opposite, 41.7% vs. 44.0%. This reversal is largely
explained by dead-zone passes rather than by better negation Categories with higher positive accuracy also lose more
when negated (Spearman ρ = 0.94 across the six categories). A larger drop therefore does not by itself show that a
category is harder to negate; it may partly reflect a higher starting point (see Appendix E.2 for more details).
     Two caveats apply when reading these comparisons. First, the size of a model’s drop does not rank its negation
ability, because the drop is capped by the model’s accuracy on the positive statement: FLUX.2-klein-9B scores 72.8%
at 1:0 and loses 43.8 points at 0:1, while Janus-Pro-7B scores 55.2%, loses 0.4, and still reaches the highest negated
accuracy. A model that is weak on the assertion has less to lose on the denial, so only the within-model comparison
is safe to read. Second, N EG T2IB ENCH measures whether a negated attribute or relation is absent from the image,
not whether the model understood the negation. A negated statement is easier to satisfy by chance than a positive one,
so a model that ignores the negation can still pass some negated statements. A control prompt that omits the tested
attribute or relation would separate the two; it fits the current design and is left for future work (Appendix F).
     Failure analysis. Every failed statement falls into one of five causes: undetected object, over-count, positive
violation, negated violation, or dead-zone failure, defined in Appendix C.3. Of the 280,067 failed statements, negated
violations are the largest group at 41.5%. Positive violations follow at 25.9%, then over-counts at 15.0%, undetected
objects at 12.6%, and dead-zone failures at 5.0%. The most common failure is a model rendering what the prompt
forbade. Dead zones, the only cause attributable to the evaluator alone, are the smallest at 5.0%. Within the same
prompt, negated statements fail more often than positive ones (Figure 3(a)). At 1:1, the negated statement causes 43%
of failures and the positive one 26%; at 1:2, the two negated statements cause 56% and the positive one 14%, twice as
much per statement. Per statement, the imbalance holds in all four mixed settings, by a factor of 1.3 to 2.0. The share
of negated violations also varies by category (Figure 3(b)): they account for 61% of failures for color and 43% for
material, but 34 to 41% for the four relations. Negated colors are thus most often rendered, resulting in an incorrect
image. Polarity therefore changes which constraint a model breaks, not only how often it breaks one. Appendix E.2
gives the per-model and row-level breakdowns.

6    Conclusion
We introduced N EG T2IB ENCH, a benchmark that measures whether T2I models can withhold what a prompt forbids
while still rendering every object it names. It compares positive and negated prompts at matched complexity and object
counts, guards against success by omission, and reduces accidental success. Its detector-based evaluator agrees with
human labels comparably to much larger vision-language judges while using a fraction of their GPU memory, and,
unlike these judges, does not depend on how the evaluation question is phrased. Across eleven T2I configurations and
211,200 generated images, nine of eleven score lower on a single negated statement than on a single positive one, with
a loss that depends on the category and compounds as statements are combined. Polarity also changes which model
leads and which error it makes. Generating what a prompt asks for and withholding what it forbids are thus distinct

A.1    Prompt construction and representative examples
Polarity settings and object-count controls. The eight settings are pos1 neg0, pos2 neg0, pos0 neg1, pos0 neg2,
pos1 neg1, pos1 neg2, pos2 neg1, and pos2 neg2. A prompt contains M = P + N statements, each assign-
ing an attribute to an object or specifying a relation between two objects. At a fixed statement load, polarity varies
while the number of statements remains constant: for example, pos2 neg0, pos1 neg1, and pos0 neg2 each
contain two statements. Attribute prompts name one object per statement. Relation prompts use a star topology with
one shared subject A and distinct targets B1 , . . . , BM , giving exactly M + 1 named objects. Thus, within each cate-
gory, comparisons at equal statement load also preserve object count. Negating an attribute or relation never removes
its objects: “a non-wooden bench” still requires a bench, and “a bicycle that is not left of a car” still requires both
objects.
    Taxonomy scope. The object vocabulary follows the detector-compatible COCO classes; prompts exclude person,
leaving 79 eligible classes. Size is treated as a comparative relation because it is defined relative to another object,
rather than as an intrinsic attribute.
    Templates and candidate selection. Prompts are generated by deterministic templates (examples in Table 4).
Attribute prompts name each object with its value: “a photo of a ⟨value⟩ ⟨object⟩” for a positive statement and “a
photo of a non-⟨value⟩ ⟨object⟩” for a negated one. Relation prompts use the star topology: “a photo of a ⟨subject⟩
and a ⟨target1 ⟩ . . . , where the ⟨subject⟩ is [not] ⟨relation⟩ the ⟨targeti ⟩”, with one clause per statement.
    The LLM selector, Qwen/Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507, filters object/value/predicate combinations
from fixed vocabularies to those judged plausible or unusual but depictable in their positive form. This ensures that
the denied attribute or relation is a meaningful possibility for the selected objects. Prompt strings are generated de-
terministically after selection, so wording does not drift across polarity conditions. Negation is then written into the
prompt itself, and the selector plays no role in the wording or in the evaluation.
    For relation construction, the selector receives a shared subject, a candidate pool of target objects, and the allowed
predicates for that category. It returns only allowed predicate-target pairs. Attribute construction analogously selects
values from the fixed color/material vocabularies. Returned values are validated before entering the corpus, and
selector outputs are cached and reused. Among valid combinations, balancing takes priority over selector preference.
Relation subjects and targets have separate usage counters; candidate targets are drawn from the least-used portion of
the vocabulary and under-used predicates are preferred. Attribute objects and values follow the analogous policy.
    Category vocabularies and sources. Table 1 lists the complete vocabularies; every value or relation can be
positive or negated. The color list is retained from GenEval [3]. The four material values are selected from the broader
OVAD vocabulary [59]. Structured prompts follow GenEval’s metadata schema and PosEval’s relation encoding [25].
The category-specific evaluation rules are defined in Table 14 and Appendix C.1.

A.2    Dataset statistics and balancing
The final star-topology corpus contains 4,800 prompts: six categories, eight settings, and 100 prompts in every
category-setting cell. Each category has 800 prompts and covers all 79 eligible COCO object classes. Averaged
over the eight settings, a prompt contains 2.25 statements, half positive and half negated. Attribute prompts name one
object per statement, giving 2.25 objects per prompt; relation prompts name one shared subject plus one target per
statement, giving 3.25 objects per prompt.
    As shown in Figures 5 and 6, all 79 eligible COCO object classes appear in every category. Pooled over the eight
settings, individual object counts remain close to the most-even allocation achievable for the corresponding number of
draws, and the smaller predicate/value vocabularies are distributed approximately uniformly.

B     Human study and evaluator validation
B.1    Reference labels and human inter-annotator score
In this study, 36 annotators labeled 600 images, three per image, using the interface in Figure 7. The reference for an
element is the majority of the three answers. The binary image label is true only when every element of the image is
satisfied, and the continuous image score is the fraction of satisfied elements. We estimate the inter-annotator score (H.
Inter-annotator) with a leave-one-out scheme: each annotator’s labels are scored against the majority of the other two,
and the score is averaged over the three held-out annotators. Table 5 reports agreement between annotators: Fleiss’
κ is 72.4 over all statements, and 84.9% of statements are answered unanimously. Agreement is lower on negated
statements (κ = 60.2, 70.2% unanimous) than on affirmative ones (73.0, 88.3%), so negation is harder for people as
well.
    The inter-annotator score is a conservative reference, not an upper bound on achievable agreement. Each held-out
annotator is compared with the majority of two annotators, a noisier target than the three-annotator majority used for
every evaluator. An evaluator above κ = 65.6 therefore agrees with the three-annotator consensus more closely than
a single annotator agrees with the other two; this does not make it more reliable than a human.

B.2    Evaluators’ scores
    • Similarity scores (CLIPScore [24], BLIPScore [44], NegationCLIP). Cosine similarity between the image em-
      bedding and the embedding of the full prompt. NegationCLIP is the negation-aware CLIP of Park et al. [9]
      (ViT-B/32).
    • Reward models (ImageReward [45], PickScore [46], HPSv2 [47]). The scalar the model assigns to the image
      and prompt. These models predict which image a person prefers, not whether the image satisfies the prompt.

    • VQA judge, whole prompt. The probability of “yes” to one question asking whether the image matches the

    • N EG T2IB ENCH. The fraction of elements its detectors mark satisfied, on the same scale as the per-element
      judges and the human score.

either answer about the whole prompt at once, which generally gives lower binary agreement (Table 6), or invent its
own decomposition. N EG T2IB ENCH also reports which requirement failed and why, which enables the category and
polarity analyses of Section 5.

B.5    Error structure
Cohen’s κ merges two kinds of error into one number. Table 9 separates them for the per-element mode, the mode
behind the precision and recall of Table 2. TP: the annotators and the evaluator both accept the image; TN: both reject
it; FP: the annotators reject it but the evaluator passes it; FN: the annotators accept it but the evaluator rejects it. Prec.
= TP/(TP + FP) and Rec. = TP/(TP + FN). Called correct (%) corresponds to the share of the 600 images the
evaluator passes, (TP + FP)/600. Humans accept 34.3% of the images.
     N EG T2IB ENCH accepts 36.8%, making 46 false passes and 31 false failures, while the strongest judge, Qwen3-
VL-32B, accepts 33.5% and makes 32 and 37. The two evaluators reject about as many images that humans accept, and
N EG T2IB ENCH passes more images that humans reject. N EG T2IB ENCH therefore rarely fails an image that humans
consider correct, so the generator failures we report are not caused by a strict evaluator. The same table exposes a
degenerate baseline that its aggregate score hides: mPLUG, scored per element, accepts only 8.8% of images and
makes 170 false failures, close to a constant “no”.

B.6    Sensitivity to how a requirement is phrased
The pos. and neg. columns of Table 2 report κ on the benchmark’s own elements and on the same elements with
the negated statements asked in negated form. Every judge loses agreement when using negated prompts. The six
instruction-tuned judges, from two model families, lose 7.8 to 18.8 points, and scale does not protect them: the two
largest judges lose 7.8 and 16.9. N EG T2IB ENCH checks a typed requirement rather than a sentence, so it is unaffected,
and under negated phrasing it ranks first.
     Table 10 shows the mechanism. For each element we ask both the affirmative and the negated form and measure
how often the answer flips. A judge that reads the negation should flip every time. Qwen3-VL-32B flips on 77.5% of
elements. Qwen2.5-VL-7B flips on only 40.7%, and its complementarity error of 0.589 shows that it mostly repeats
its affirmative answer.

C     Evaluation rules and metrics
C.1    Evaluation rules
All reported benchmark results use RF-DETR-XL with the current evaluator configuration. The formal rules below
are deterministic. Unless otherwise noted, only relation measurement operates on a SAM-2-refined instance mask.
    Detection and instance counting. RF-DETR-XL (RFDETRSegXLarge) is called with an internal prediction
floor of 0.005. A requested class is considered present when at least one detection reaches

                                                    τpresence = 0.03.

Instance counting uses the stricter threshold.
                                                      τcount = 0.5.
If no detection reaches τcount , the most confident detection for that requested class is retained so that a faint but
detected object can still be judged. If more counted instances of a requested class are present than requested, the
prompt is marked as an over-count. skis is exempt from this rule because one semantic referent is often returned as
multiple detections. At most 16 detections per class are retained, and overlap suppression is disabled (IoU = 1.0).

passes because the denied predicate is absent. Proximity has no dead zone by construction because its two predicates
are exact complements. Attribute confusion sets are also never empty because they always include the top-scoring
value.

E     Generator experiments
E.1    Experimental setup
Models and sample counts. We evaluate eleven model configurations on the full N EG T2IB ENCH corpus: BLIP3-o
NEXT SFT 3B [19], FLUX.2-klein-9B and FLUX.2-dev (4-bit) [13], HiDream-I1 (4-bit) [15], HunyuanImage-2.1
(distilled) [17], Janus-Pro-7B [18], Qwen-Image [14], SD 3.5 Large [12], Z-Image-Turbo [16], and SD 1.5 [10] with
and without the negation-aware text encoder of Park et al. [9]. Each configuration generates four samples for the same
4,800 prompts, yielding 19,200 evaluated images per configuration and 211,200 images in total.
    Generation settings. All model configurations use the same N EG T2IB ENCH prompt files and four samples per
prompt with seeds 42, . . . , 45. We intentionally use each pipeline’s native generation configuration rather than forcing
an equal inference-compute budget. Consequently, absolute between-model comparisons also reflect differences in
inference schedule and output resolution; within-model comparisons across benchmark settings do not.
    No model-specific prompt engineering is applied, and the CFG negative-prompt field is unused. The SD 1.5 and
SD 1.5+NegationCLIP entries share the same base checkpoint and 50-step schedule, but their registered guidance
scales are 9.0 and 7.5 respectively; they should therefore not be interpreted as a perfectly controlled encoder interven-

E.2    Additional results
Failure analysis. The main paper reports failure composition by benchmark setting and semantic category. Here we
provide the corresponding statement-count calculation and additional image- and model-level diagnostics. Across the
full benchmark there are 475,200 statement evaluations. For one prompt from each of the eight settings, the total
statement load is
                                      1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 4 = 18.
With six categories, 100 prompts per category–setting cell, four generated samples per prompt, and eleven model
configurations, this gives
                                       6 × 100 × 4 × 11 × 18 = 475,200
statement evaluations. Of these, 280,067 are failed statements; the statement-level percentages in Section 5 are com-
puted over these failed statements, or over the corresponding failed statements within each setting or category.
    At the image level, 158,421 of 211,200 generations are incorrect. Among these, 40.1% contain only negated-
statement violations, 25.9% only positive-statement violations, and 13.9% violations of both polarities. Over-counting
accounts for a further 10.5% and undetected objects for 9.6%. Images that violate both positive and negated statements
are retained as a separate category rather than assigned to either polarity.
    Dead-zone analysis. Directional, comparative, and depth relations contain a deliberate dead zone: when the
measured geometry is insufficient to support any predicate in the category, the evaluator returns an empty predicate set
rather than forcing a decision. This can be the correct outcome; for example, two objects at approximately the same
depth may be neither clearly in front of nor behind one another.
    Let r denote the relation predicate a statement names (e.g., left of ) and R(A, B) the set of predicates the evaluator
returns for the object pair (A, B). A positive statement passes if and only if r ∈ R(A, B), whereas a negated statement
passes if and only if r ∈ / R(A, B). Consequently, when R(A, B) = ∅, a positive statement fails while a negated
statement passes. We call the latter a dead-zone pass. Such a pass is valid under the benchmark rule, but differs from
a pass in which an alternative predicate is positively established.
    Across the full experiment, 158,400 negated relation statements are evaluated across the 211,200 generated images.
Of these, shown in Table 19, 37,785 are unevaluable because an endpoint is missing or over-counted. Among the re-
maining evaluable statements, 62,790 pass, and 11,907 of those passes (19.0%) arise from the dead zone. Equivalently,
dead-zone passes account for 7.5% of all negated relation statements.
    The effect is concentrated in depth: 55.5% of successful negated depth statements occur when no depth predicate
is resolved. This does not imply that those images are incorrectly scored; an object pair may genuinely lack a clear
front–behind ordering. It does mean, however, that such cases provide weaker evidence of negation following than
cases in which the evaluator positively establishes the alternative relation.
    Proximity provides a useful contrast. Its near/far predicates are exact complements under a single decision bound-
ary, so it has no designed dead zone. The three observed empty-set cases are degenerate geometry rather than a
consequence of the decision rule.
    Negation-aware text encoder. Replacing SD 1.5’s text encoder with NegationCLIP changes overall image accu-
racy only from 14.5% to 14.9%. Both SD 1.5 configurations are unusual in scoring higher on pos0 neg1 than on
pos1 neg0, but the pair remains well below the other configurations overall. This comparison is not fully controlled:
the checkpoint and 50-step schedule are shared, but the registered guidance scales differ (9.0 vs. 7.5). We therefore
treat the pair as suggestive rather than as an isolated causal test of the text encoder.

F     Limitations
N EG T2IB ENCH is limited to what can be verified automatically in both polarities: color, material, and four spatial and
size relations over 79 COCO classes. Contact, interactions such as holding or riding, and the negated presence of whole
objects are not covered, and are natural extensions as detectors improve. Comparative relations also measure apparent
image size rather than real-world size. Negation is expressed in one English form (“non-value”, “is not relation”),
and relation prompts link one subject to every target. This keeps polarity comparisons controlled, but robustness to
paraphrases, other languages, and richer relation structures is not tested; these can be added on the same evaluation
pipeline.
    N EG T2IB ENCH measures whether negated content is absent from the image, not whether the model understood
the negation. Because negated statements are easier to satisfy by chance than positive ones, a model that ignores a
negation can still pass some negated statements. A control prompt that omits the tested attribute or relation would
separate the two, and fits the current design. Scores depend on off-the-shelf perception models (RF-DETR, SAM 2,
SigLIP 2, and Depth-Anything V2), so benchmark accuracy is bounded by their quality; the modular design lets
each component be replaced as stronger models appear. Finally, we evaluate open-weight models under their native
inference settings rather than a matched compute budget; closed systems can be scored with the same protocol.
